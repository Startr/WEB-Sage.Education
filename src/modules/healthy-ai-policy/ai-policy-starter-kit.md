## Starter Kit

**Purpose:** A takeaway summary a school team can print, put on a wall, and carry into practice. It is **not the policy itself** — it points to the essential decisions and language drawn from the four-part module. *This kit uses the module's default words — substitute your §1.2 definitions and Activity 1-A mindset terms throughout.*

---

## The mindset axis — one axis, four framings

| Framing | Healthy pole | Unhealthy pole |
|---------|--------------|----------------|
| Maker vs. Consumer | Maker (author; AI is material) | Consumer (audience; submits AI output) |
| Slow AI vs. Fast AI | Slow (learner in charge; AI partner) | Fast (outsources thinking) |
| Conscious vs. Reflexive | Conscious (deliberate, aware choice) | Reflexive (automatic, unexamined) |
| User vs. Author | Author (owns thinking and work) | User (consumes ready-made thinking) |

**The through-line:** the learner stays **in charge of the ideas**; AI is a **partner/material**; use is **conscious and explained**. *In Activity 1-A, choose a lead pair + an everyday pair; recognize all four.*

## Shared definitions

- **AI** — technology that performs tasks needing human intelligence: pattern recognition, prediction, decision support. *(Adaptive learning, spell/grammar checkers.)*
- **Generative AI (GAI)** — a type of AI that **creates** new text/image/audio/video/code in response to prompts — outputs can contain confident errors (confabulations); check against trusted sources. *(ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, DALL·E.)*

## The AI Integration Continuum (for assignments & units)

| Level | What it means | Example |
|-------|---------------|---------|
| **No-AI** | AI not part of the work by design | First draft, reflection, observation |
| **AI-Resistant** | Designed so AI **cannot** complete it | In-class oral defence, hand-drawn artefact, live demo |
| **AI-Optional** | May use AI at the student's discretion within teacher-set boundaries; student explains the choice — including choosing none | Task where students decide whether to use AI for support, and reflect on the decision |
| **AI-Required** | Students use AI, then **critique/extend/build on** it; submit AI output **+ their judgment** | Compare an AI draft to your own; critique it |
| **AI-Collaborative** | AI is a working partner; student decides moment-to-moment **and explains the decisions** | Research task managed with AI; explain two leaning-in/leaning-out choices |
| **AI-Building** | The work is the AI itself — students **design, build, test, and refine** their own chatbot/agent on an approved platform | Build a study-buddy bot; test it; fix what it gets wrong; submit build log + reflection |

**Design principle:** a healthy unit **mixes levels deliberately** — balance is the goal, not maximal AI.

## What is AI plagiarism?

**Submitting AI-generated content as your own work without disclosure, citation, or meaningful engagement.** Includes: submitting AI output uncredited, paraphrasing/rewriting to evade detection, and fabricating sources/data with AI.

**Healthy use (not plagiarism):** transparent, disclosed use; the learner keeps the thinking and judgement; AI's contribution is named.

## Disclosure — the "when, why, how" AI use statement

> **AI use statement:** I used [tool] to [task]. I set it aside when [reason]. My own thinking is in [what I judged/revised/created]. I did not use AI for [what I must defend myself].

**Rule of thumb:** If AI meaningfully contributed to your content or ideas (quotes, paraphrases, images, data, structures, drafting, substantial editing), **acknowledge and cite it**. Routine grammar tools need little or none. AI is a **tool/source, never an author**. Use a shareable link to the conversation where possible. *(Cite per your school's chosen style — APA, MLA, Chicago, etc.)*

## School-level essentials (from Part 2)

- **Vision:** students and teachers are makers, not consumers; AI serves learning and wellbeing.
- **Audience responsibilities:** students disclose and follow assignment levels; teachers design balanced units and teach disclosure; parents support healthy use at home.
- **Teachers decide, the policy communicates:** teachers decide how, when, and why AI shows up in their classroom; the policy makes those decisions visible to students, parents, and staff.
- **Health test:** healthy AI keeps the essential thinking with the learner; unhealthy AI outsources it.
- **Integrity:** favour clarity and conversation over surveillance; design AI-Resistant assessment for high stakes; know the three incident tiers and the contact sheet (§2.6).
- **Privacy & safety:** approved tools only (new tools go through the §2.5 approval process); no private information in public AI (grade-banded baseline); review every tool before adoption (tool-review checklist); teacher-led use in elementary.
- **Review:** revisit every 6–12 months; watch agreed signals between reviews and trigger off-cycle review after a serious incident, vendor change, or regulation change. Align to your jurisdiction (provincial/state/national/EU rules) and board policy.

## Classroom-level essentials (from Part 4)

- **Prepare staff first** — run the Step 0 tasting session (try levels as learners; calibrate disclosure) before co-creating with students (§4.2).
- **Co-create with students** — write 4–6 norms in the students' voice (e.g., "We use AI to help us think, not to think for us.")
- **Privacy norms** — never type private info (names, addresses, phone numbers, passwords, school IDs, photos, medical/confidential items, others' writing) into AI; ask when unsure; extend by grade band (§2.4).
- **Elementary framing** — "AI is not a person"; teacher-led use; stoplight (Stop / Ask first / Use together); short rules + reflection card (§4.4).
- **Same frame as the school** — classroom policy links back to the school-wide policy; never contradicts it.
- **Same disclosure** — use the school's chosen AI use statement / citation style.
- **Tell parents plainly** — a short letter explaining the levels, disclosure, and "What was your thinking here?"

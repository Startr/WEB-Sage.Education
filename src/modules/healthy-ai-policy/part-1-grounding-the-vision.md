## 1.1 Start with questions, not answers

A healthy AI policy begins the way good teaching does: with questions. Resist the urge to open a blank document and start writing rules. Instead, slow down and let your team surface what it actually believes, worries about, and hopes for.

**Clarifying questions for your leadership team** (work through these out loud or in a shared doc):

**About your school and community**
- Who is our community, and what do they most need and fear when it comes to AI?
- What are the ages and developmental stages of our students, and how does that shape what is appropriate?
- What does our community value most — what is at the centre of our school's mission?
- Who must be at the table when we decide (teachers, students, parents, trustees, unions, IT, legal)?

**About AI itself**
- What do we mean when we say "AI"? What do we mean by "generative AI" (GAI)?
- Which AI tools are already being used in our building, by staff and students — whether or not we know it?
- What do we actually know, versus assume, about these tools?
- What is the difference between a tool that *analyses* and one that *creates*, and why does that difference matter for learning?
- How will we decide which AI tools are approved for school use, and who keeps that list honest?

**About healthy use**
- What does "using AI in a healthy and balanced way" mean *in our school*, concretely?
- Where does AI genuinely help learning, and where does it quietly replace the thinking students need to do?
- How do we want our students and teachers to *feel* about using AI — dependent, empowered, anxious, confident?
- What would "unhealthy" AI use look like here, and how would we recognise it early?

**About policy and its limits**
- What is a policy *for* here — protection, permission, or both?
- What already exists in our jurisdiction (Canada: provincial/territorial guidance; USA: state and district guidance; EU: GDPR and the AI Act) that shapes what we can and should say?
- How will we keep a policy from going stale as tools change within months?
- Who is accountable for revisiting and updating it?
- When AI causes harm — a harmful output, a breach, serious misuse — who responds first, and how?

**Capture your answers.** At the end, the team should be able to answer clearly: *In our school, AI use is healthy when it ______ and unhealthy when it ______.*

## 1.2 Agree on shared definitions: AI vs. Generative AI (GAI)

A common source of confusion in draft policies is using terms without defining them — or defining them without examples. Before writing policy, agree on plain, working definitions the whole school can use.

**Artificial intelligence (AI)** — A broad field of technology in which computer systems perform tasks that normally require human intelligence: recognising patterns, making predictions, and supporting decisions. Traditional AI is mostly **analytical and predictive** — it interprets data and helps a system decide.

- *Examples in schools:* adaptive learning platforms that adjust difficulty to a student's level; recommendation systems; spell-checkers and grammar tools; plagiarism checkers; early-warning systems that flag students who may need support.

**Generative AI (GAI)** — A subset of AI that **creates new content** — text, images, audio, video, code — in response to a prompt, rather than only analyzing existing data. It is trained on large bodies of data and can produce fluent, original-looking output (and sometimes plausible-sounding errors, called "hallucinations" or better defined as "confabulations"). *Why confabulation: "hallucination" suggests perceiving something unreal — a human, almost intentional act. These systems don't perceive; they generate the most plausible continuation of a pattern, confidently and with no intent to deceive. "Confabulation" names exactly that — which is why checking output against trusted sources matters.*

- *Examples in schools:* ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, DALL·E, Midjourney, GitHub Copilot.

**The distinction that matters for learning:** Traditional AI mostly *supports and personalises* (it helps a learner or a system do a task more effectively), while generative AI *produces the artefact itself*. This is why GAI raises different questions about authorship, plagiarism, and cognitive effort than AI that merely tailors content.

**Coaching note.** If teachers and students cannot look at a tool and say whether it is AI or generative AI — and what each is *for* — then the policy built on those terms will fail. This section, and Part 3's academic-integrity work, depend on everyone sharing these definitions.

**Our working definitions (record).** Write the plain definitions your whole school will use — this record feeds the policy template in Part 2:
- In our school, **AI** means: ___
- **Generative AI** means: ___
- Examples our staff and students will recognise: ___

## 1.3 The mindset axis: maker vs. consumer

The philosophical backbone of this module is a single axis about *who* is thinking and *who* is the author of the learning and the work.

**Maker mindset** — Treating AI as a starting point to build on. The student is the **author** of the final result; AI is a material they shape.

**Consumer mindset** — Treating AI as an answer-vending machine. The student is the audience to AI's output rather than its author, and submits what AI produced.

This is the Sage.Education "Maker Mindset" framing: learners who **shape their future** rather than merely receive it, who build on AI output as a raw material rather than submitting it wholesale.

### One axis, four framings

Because different audiences — students, teachers, parents, trustees — need to meet the same idea at a level they can grasp, the module offers **four framings of the same axis**. Decide, with your team, which names you will lead with and which you will offer as plain-language equivalents. You do not need all four everywhere; you need everyone to share one axis.

| Framing | The healthy pole | The unhealthy pole | Best for |
|---------|------------------|--------------------|----------|
| **Maker vs. Consumer** | Maker — student is author; AI is material | Consumer — student is audience; submits AI output | Values and identity language; Sage.Education community; staff culture |
| **Slow AI vs. Fast AI** | Slow — learner stays in charge of ideas; AI is a collaborative partner (Beghetto & Glăveanu) | Fast — learner rushes to outsource thinking to AI | Scholarly backing; design of learning experiences; discussion of cognitive offloading |
| **Conscious vs. Reflexive** | Conscious — deliberate, aware choice of *when* to use AI and *why* | Reflexive — automatic, unexamined use of whatever AI returns | Plain-language policy; classroom norms; parent communication; metacognition |
| **User vs. Author** | Author — owns the thinking and the final work | User — consumes ready-made thinking | A complement that emphasises ownership of ideas |

**The through-line:** the healthy pole always keeps the **human in charge of the ideas**, uses AI as a **partner or material**, and is **aware of why AI is being used**. The unhealthy pole always **outsources the thinking**, treats AI as the **answer**, and reaches for it **reflexively**.

*How this module uses the four framings: maker/consumer for vision and culture; conscious/reflexive for policy and classroom norms; slow/fast when citing learning-design research; author/user when discussing ownership and integrity. You only need to choose two below — but recognize all four, so no one is confused when colleagues use different words for the same axis. Substitute your Activity 1-A choices wherever you see ours.*

**Coaching note.** Help your team pick a pair to *lead with* (we recommend **maker mindset vs. consumer mindset**) and a pair for *everyday classroom/policy language* (we recommend **conscious vs. reflexive**). The other two framings need no decision — just recognition.

**Activity 1-A — Name your axis.** Do this first — you'll need your chosen language for the domain discussions (§1.5) and the vision statement (§1.6).

1. Each member names the pair they'd lead with, and one sentence why (2 minutes each).
2. Decide together and record below: your **lead pair** and your **everyday pair**.
3. Carry this record into Parts 2–4 — every template that names a mindset term should use your words, not ours.

**Our axis, in our words (decision record)**
- We lead with: ___ vs. ___
- Everyday classroom/policy language: ___ vs. ___
- One sentence a parent, student, and teacher will each hear: "___"

*Three schools, three completions of the same map:*
- *Default (most schools): lead — maker vs. consumer; everyday — conscious vs. reflexive. Parent sentence: "In our school, students stay conscious makers of their work — AI is material they shape, not answers they submit."*
- *Elementary-led: lead — conscious vs. reflexive ("we decide first, we don't just tap"); everyday — "my own thinking" in class (maker vs. consumer lives in staff culture only). Parent sentence: "Your child learns to decide first — to think before they tap — and to make their own thinking with AI's help."*
- *Integrity-led secondary: lead — author vs. user (the entry problem was undisclosed AI work); everyday — conscious vs. reflexive. Parent sentence: "Every piece of work must be authored, not just used — students own their thinking and show where AI helped."*

*Translation check — the record's real job: say one commitment two ways. "We stay makers" (vision) = "we stay conscious" (policy). If your team can do this sentence, no one will be confused when colleagues use different words.*

## 1.4 Healthy vs. unhealthy AI use: the cognitive dimension

The single most important idea in a healthy-AI policy is **what happens to the thinking**. Research on **cognitive offloading** is the strongest grounding here.

**Beneficial (healthy) offloading** — A learner uses AI to relieve *routine or repetitive* load (e.g., formatting, finding sources, checking grammar) while keeping the **essential thinking** — the reasoning, judging, and creating — in their own hands. The learner owns the outcome.

**Harmful (unhealthy) offloading** — A learner uses AI to *outsource the very cognitive work the task exists to develop* (e.g., getting the answer to a maths problem instead of solving it, or accepting AI-generated writing without meaning-making). This produces strong short-term *performance* but damages **long-term learning**.

Evidence relevant to your conversations:
- Students who used AI to get the answer in high-school maths **suffered harm to long-term learning** (Bastani et al., 2025).
- Learners who **actively modified** AI-generated text improved; learners who **passively accepted** it declined (Yang et al., 2025).
- Research distinguishes **dependent** offloading (cedes cognitive authority to AI; linked to lower creativity, deep processing, and independent judgment) from **autonomous** offloading (keeps the learner in charge; linked to positive outcomes).
- Frequent AI use correlates with reduced critical thinking, and this effect runs through increased cognitive offloading and cognitive fatigue.

> **[Verified]** Sources reviewed this run: cognitive-offloading synthesis covering active modification vs. passive acceptance (Yang et al., 2025), answer-seeking harms in maths (Bastani et al., 2025), the critical-thinking correlation (Gerlich, 2025), dependent vs. autonomous offloading, and AI-dependence/cognitive-fatigue findings. See `99_research_log.md` §3.

**Coaching note.** Frame this positively for staff: healthy use is not "less AI" — it is **the right kind of AI use for the learning goal**. The AI Integration Continuum in Part 3 is built precisely so teachers can mix levels of AI deliberately based on what a lesson is trying to teach.

## 1.5 Six domains of benefit and deficit

A balanced school mindset considers **both** the benefits and the deficits of AI use. Presenting AI as pure promise produces hype policy; presenting it as pure threat produces ban policy. Instead, read the **five domains** below — each describing what AI use can open up and what it can erode — and, importantly, *how that picture shifts by grade level, task, and student need.*

Read each passage with your team, then complete Activity 1-B: name the risks that worry you most and the benefits you want most.

Note that Domain 6 answers a different question — *how much AI is designed into the task* — and is previewed, not discussed, here.

### Domain 1 — Cognitive benefit ↔ deficit
What happens to learning and thinking?

AI extends thinking when it scaffolds — formatting, finding sources, checking grammar — while the reasoning, judging, and creating stay with the learner. Students who actively reshape AI output improve; students who accept it unchanged decline. It damages thinking when it performs the very work the task exists to develop: maths answers that bypass solving, prose accepted without meaning-making — strong short-term performance, fragile long-term learning. And it opens genuinely new ground: translation that unlocks foreign materials, simulated practice, instant feedback on demand. *Where in your school is AI currently scaffolding — and where has it quietly become the thinker?*

### Domain 2 — Psychological / emotional benefit ↔ deficit
How does AI use *feel* and what does it do to well-being?

AI steadies learners when they choose it deliberately and it lowers the cost of trying — guided practice that builds confident control, mastery experiences that strengthen self-belief. It harms when use turns reflexive: dependence that feels like helplessness, constant algorithmic measurement that raises anxiety, hours with platforms instead of people. Familiarity itself matters — unfamiliar tools lower confidence and positive feeling, while trained, supported use restores it. And connection counts: AI serves well-being when it frees time for human relationship, not when it replaces it. *Do your students and teachers feel more capable with AI — or more watched by it?*

### Domain 3 — Social / relational benefit ↔ deficit
What does AI do to relationships and community?

AI serves community when it frees teacher time for the human relationship rather than replacing it, when students build with it together instead of alone on solitary platforms, and when its use is open — agreed norms, transparent practice, a shared culture of making. It corrodes when platforms displace teachers, when use goes solitary and hidden, when nobody in the building can say what the norms actually are. Collaboration itself is the anchor: human partnership first, AI as a tool inside it. *Would your students describe AI use in your school as something done together and in the open — or alone and unspoken?*

### Domain 4 — Integrity / trust benefit ↔ deficit
What does AI do to honesty and trust?

Trust grows where AI use is disclosed and cited, where authorship is clear, and where grades rest on secured evidence of real thinking — key moments, like live defences, that show whose learning a grade actually reflects. It collapses where AI output passes as a student's own — undisclosed, uncredited, or rewritten to hide its origin — and where suspicion triggers a detection arms race that poisons the very relationship it claims to protect. Transparency, not surveillance, is what makes assessment defensible across a school year. *Can you tell, for any given grade in your school, whose learning it actually shows?*

### Domain 5 — Equity / access benefit ↔ deficit
What does AI do for fairness and inclusion?

AI includes when access is equitable *and* supported: personalised scaffolds with the teacher mediating, deliberate use that removes barriers — translation, read-aloud, differentiated entry points for learners otherwise locked out. Personalisation and barrier-removal are different jobs, and schools need both. AI excludes when access is uneven and gaps widen, when one-size tools stand in for real support, and when inclusion happens by accident rather than design — ad-hoc help when someone happens to notice. *Which learners in your school does AI currently reach — and who is still standing outside it?*

### Domain 6 — Integration levels (preview of Part 3)
How much AI is deliberately designed into the learning task? (Left-to-right here is volume, not value — see the note above.)

**No-AI → AI-Resistant → AI-Optional → AI-Required → AI-Collaborative → AI-Building**

## 1.6 Drafting the shared vision statement

A vision statement is short, human, and describes the *feeling and direction* the school is moving toward with AI — not the rules (those come later).

### Vision-statement drafting template

> In our school, AI is a [tool / partner / material] that we use to support [learning / creativity / human connection / wellbeing]. We believe students and teachers are [makers, not consumers — or your Activity 1-A lead pair] of AI output — they stay [conscious — or your everyday language] and [in charge of their ideas] while using AI to [amplify their thinking]. We commit to using AI in a [healthy, balanced, equitable] way that [protects and grows the thinking our learners need], and to revisiting our understanding as the tools and our community evolve.

**Prompts to personalise it:**
- What is the one sentence you want a parent, a student, and a teacher each to remember about AI in your school?
- Does your vision lead with *protection* (guardrails), *possibility* (what AI enables), or *both*? 
- What three words describe how you want people at your school to *feel* when they use AI?

### Checklist for a strong vision statement
- [ ] Plain language — a grade-appropriate reader can grasp it.
- [ ] Names the mindset axis in your Activity 1-A words (e.g., maker/conscious/author, not consumer/reflexive/user).
- [ ] Acknowledges both benefit and deficit (not a hype statement, not a fear statement).
- [ ] Feels like *your* school, not a generic template.
- [ ] Short enough to put on a poster and in a parent letter.

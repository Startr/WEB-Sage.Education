**Audience:** School IT leads + building principals/assistant principals (K–12)
**Format:** Self-paced · Coaching toolkit · No time limit
**Purpose:** Develop and articulate the **school-wide** AI policy first, so that it sets the frame, values, and expectations that the classroom-level policy (Part 4) will align with. This part produces a draft policy the school can adapt to its own context.

> **Important framing.** This module *guides* your school to write its own policy — it does not write it for you. Every school is different in size, grade band, values, infrastructure, and regulatory environment (provincial/territorial in Canada, state/district in the USA, and national/EU regulation in Europe). Use the templates and questions here to arrive at *your* policy. Where a school has an existing draft, use Part 1's question bank to clarify and rebuild it rather than borrowing our language wholesale.

### If you sit under a board or district

Many schools need board or district adoption before a policy is enforceable. Settle this early:

- **Who adopts?** Name who approves the school-wide policy — principal, board, or district office — and whose signature makes it official.
- **Align, don't duplicate.** Check your draft against any existing board acceptable-use policy or district AI guidance. Where they overlap, the school policy implements the board frame in plain, student-friendly language — national guidance increasingly pairs formal board policy with plain-language school agreements.
- **Divergence protocol.** Where school and board rules differ, the board rule wins. Document any variance you request rather than staying silent about the gap.
- **Sync the cycles.** Align your 6–12-month review with the board's policy calendar so updates happen once, together.

## 2.1 Design principles for a healthy school-wide policy

A policy earns trust when it is clear, practical, and seen as serving learning rather than policing it. Use these principles as your test.

1. **Plain language.** Anyone — a grade-5 student, a parent, a substitute teacher — can read it and know what to do. Define every term you use (AI, generative AI, your Activity 1-A mindset terms, the continuum levels, plagiarism, disclosure).
2. **Starts from values, not fear.** It opens with the vision (Part 1), not a list of prohibitions. Rules follow from values.
3. **Distinguishes audiences.** Teachers, students, and parents have different roles and responsibilities. Separate those clearly.
4. **Names both benefit and deficit.** It should not read as hype (AI will transform everything) or fear (AI is cheating). The domains from Part 1 keep it balanced.
5. **Is flexible and time-stamped.** Tools change within months. Include an explicit review cadence (e.g., review every 6–12 months) and a commitment to update as the school learns.
6. **Aligns to your jurisdiction.** It fits within provincial/state/national and EU frameworks rather than contradicting them.
7. **Communicates teacher decisions.** Teachers are the classroom professionals who decide **how, when, and why** AI shows up in their classroom. The policy does not make those decisions for them — it **communicates** them clearly to students, parents, and staff.

### A note on regulation (context to verify locally)
Schools in different regions sit under different frameworks. Confirm which apply to your school:
- **Canada:** provincial/territorial education ministries; PIPEDA (and provincial privacy laws like Quebec's Law 25) for student data. Some provinces (e.g., Quebec) have issued AI-related education guidance.
- **USA:** state education agencies and district policies shape AI guidance; FERPA and COPPA govern student-data privacy.
- **Europe / EU:** GDPR governs personal data; the EU AI Act is the first comprehensive AI regulation and categorises AI systems by risk; individual member states and regions add their own guidance.
- **International reference points** the policy can align with (not law, but credibility): the **ISTE Standards** (students, educators, leaders), **OECD** guidance urging adaptive, forward-looking frameworks rather than rigid bans, **UNESCO** guidance on generative AI in education, and the joint **EC–OECD AILit Framework** (Engage with AI, Create with AI, Manage AI, Shape AI).

> **[Verified]** Sources reviewed in this run: ISTE Standards and AI resources (iste.org); OECD Digital Education Outlook 2023 (oecd.org) recommending adaptive guidance and human-rights-based, equitable use; the EC–OECD AILit Framework (ailiteracyframework.org); UNESCO's Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research (referenced). Local rules vary — verify before adopting.

### [School Name] — Healthy Use of Artificial Intelligence Policy
**Version [X] · Review date: [date] · Source: [vision statement version]**

#### 1. Why we have this policy
At [school name], we believe AI can help people learn, create, and connect — and that we must use it in a healthy, balanced way. Our goal is that every student and teacher stays the **author of their own thinking**, using AI as a **partner and material**, not as a replacement for learning. *(Roots in Part 1 vision.)*
We use AI **consciously** and with a **maker mindset**. That means:
- We stay **in charge of our ideas**, and we can say *why* we used AI for a task (or why we chose not to).
- We treat AI as a **partner or material**, not an answer-vending machine.
- We **disclose** when and how we used AI, and we take ownership of our own learning.

#### 2. What we mean by AI
*Start from your §1.2 working-definitions record — paste it here and edit as needed.*
- **AI (artificial intelligence):** technology that performs tasks that normally need human intelligence — recognising patterns, making predictions, supporting decisions. *Examples: adaptive learning platforms, recommendation tools, spell/grammar checkers.*
- **Generative AI (GAI):** a type of AI that **creates new content** — text, images, audio, video, code — in response to a prompt. *Examples: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, DALL·E.*
- **Maker mindset:** the student/teacher is the **author** of the result; AI is a starting point or material to build on.
- **Consumer mindset:** the student/teacher is the **audience** of AI's output and submits it as-is.
- **AI plagiarism:** submitting AI-generated work as your own without disclosure, citation, or meaningful engagement — including rewriting it to hide its origin. *(Elementary: passing off AI work as your own. Full definition: Part 3.)*
- *(Replace with your Activity 1-A decision: lead with your chosen pair, and give the everyday equivalent alongside.)*

#### 3. What healthy use looks like
Healthy use of AI at our school means using it in a way that **protects and grows the thinking** a task is meant to develop:
- using AI to handle routine or repetitive work while we keep the essential thinking and judgement;
- building on AI output critically rather than accepting it without thought;
- stopping AI use where it would replace the learning our progress depends on (see Part 3 continuum: No-AI and AI-Resistant assignments);
- checking AI output for errors and bias, since GAI can be wrong.

#### 4. What unhealthy use looks like
Unhealthy use means using AI in a way that **replaces our learning**:
- submitting AI-generated work as our own without disclosure or citation (see Part 3 on AI plagiarism);
- using AI to **skip** the thinking a task exists to develop;
- using AI reflexively, as a default answer machine rather than a conscious choice;
- endangering privacy, safety, or fairness.

We treat **undisclosed** AI-generated work as academic dishonesty — **AI plagiarism** *(elementary: passing off AI work as your own)* — and we teach **disclosure and citation** as a skill (Part 3). We favour clear expectations and honesty over surveillance, and we review work through conversation, not only detection.

**School decisions → Appendices.** Record your approved tools & platforms (Appendix A; how-to §2.5), privacy/data rules (Appendix B; baseline below), age-appropriate access (Appendix C; §2.4), consequences (conduct code), and incident tiers + contacts (Appendix D; §2.6) — adapted to your jurisdiction and infrastructure.

#### 5. Responsibilities by audience

**Students** will:
- use AI with a maker mindset and disclose when/how they used it;
- follow their teacher's assignment-level guidance (No-AI, AI-Resistant, AI-Optional, AI-Required, AI-Collaborative, AI-Building);
- produce work that shows their own thinking and learning.

**Teachers** will:
- decide **how, when, and why** AI shows up in their classroom, using this policy to make those decisions visible to students and parents;
- design lessons and units with a deliberate, balanced mix of AI use (Part 3 continuum);
- make AI expectations clear for each assignment and model and teach good disclosure and citation;
- protect time and activities where AI is not used, so students develop and show their own skill;
- support students' wellbeing, not just their productivity, when using AI.
- use AI for grading, reports, parent communication, and planning only on **approved, school-managed tools** — never entering student work, records, or personal data into public AI tools; review every AI-assisted word as their own professional judgement; and disclose AI assistance to parents where it shapes grades or formal reports.
- complete the school's AI induction (Step 0, Part 4) on joining and revisit it in each review cycle's refresh.

**Parents/guardians** will:
- understand the school's AI expectations and support their child in using AI healthily at home;
- receive clear communication about when and how AI is used in schoolwork;
- reach out to the school with questions rather than using AI to do their child's thinking for them.

#### 6. Review
This policy will be reviewed every **[6–12 months]** by **[owner/role]** and updated as tools and our community evolve. Updates will be communicated to students, staff, and parents. Between reviews, **[owner/role]** watches agreed signals — help-desk tickets, disclosure quality, the incident log, parent questions — and runs a short each-term pulse with staff. A serious incident, vendor change, or regulation change triggers an off-cycle review.

### Coaching questions to tailor the template
- **Grade bands:** How should expectations differ for elementary, middle, and high school students? (Consider Appendix C — see the §2.4 baseline and checklist.)
- **Access & equity:** Which AI tools do we fund, permit, and support so that every student has fair access? Do we have a privacy-first, local/self-hosted option (as some privacy-conscious schools do)?
- **Tools:** Which tools are approved schoolwide? Which require specific oversight? (See the §2.4 baseline and checklist.)
- **Staff use:** Which staff tasks (grading, reports, parent communication, planning) may use AI, on which approved tools, and what must staff review and disclose?
- **Assessment:** Which assignments in our school are No-AI or AI-Resistant by design, so students can demonstrate skill without AI (Part 3)?
- **Wellbeing:** How do we support the emotional side — reducing AI-related anxiety and performance pressure (Part 1, Domain 2)?
- **Infrastructure:** Do we provide AI accounts/platforms, training, and support? Who manages them?
- **Workload & representation:** What new workload does this policy create (assessment redesign, disclosure teaching, calibration), and have staff representatives / unions been consulted so expectations are enforceable and resourced?
- **Board/district:** Who adopts our policy, and how do we handle divergence from board or district rules?
- **Additional needs:** How do IEP/504 teams decide on AI as accommodation, and how do we stay within age and consent rules for our youngest learners?
- **Incidents:** Who is our safeguarding/data lead and vendor contact, and what is the first-hour response when AI causes harm? (See §2.6.)

## 2.3 Aligning school-wide policy to the classroom level (preview)

Your classroom policy (Part 4) will be built **from** this school-wide policy. Two design moves keep them aligned:

1. **Radiate the same values downward.** The school-wide policy sets the *frame* (maker mindset, disclosure, the continuum, healthy vs. unhealthy). The classroom policy fills in the *specifics* for one class: what the teacher's assignments will be, how students will disclose, and the norms the class agrees on together.
2. **Keep a single source of truth.** A classroom policy should link back to and never contradict the school-wide document. When the school-wide policy changes, classroom policies update to match.

**Reflection for your team:** *If a teacher walks into a classroom on the first day, what do they need from the school-wide policy to confidently co-create a classroom policy with their students?* Ensure Part 2 gives them that.

## 2.4 Age-appropriate use, privacy, and safety (school-wide decisions)

> **[Adapted with attribution]** — The age-appropriate-use guidance, tool-review checklist, and privacy/safety baselines in this section adapt (paraphrase, not copy) the elementary and privacy materials from Med Kharbach's *Classroom AI Use & Disclosure Agreement* (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). See `99_research_log.md` for attribution.

Healthy AI policy protects more than thinking — it protects people. Put two pieces in the school-wide policy itself: **age-appropriate use** decisions (especially for elementary) and a **privacy/safety baseline** that every classroom policy builds on.

### Age-appropriate use
In elementary classrooms, AI use should be **teacher-led or school-approved** rather than a student-invoked default. Many public AI tools carry age, consent, or account restrictions, so students should not be expected to use public AI tools independently. Introduce a tool only after checking school, district, and family guidance.

Teachers may still use AI heavily **behind the scenes** — creating examples and model responses, adapting reading passages to different levels, generating discussion questions, preparing vocabulary practice, creating images/prompts/visuals, differentiating materials, and planning lessons — as long as they **review, edit, and verify every AI-generated material before it reaches students**.

For secondary, the policy should name which tools are **approved schoolwide** and which require teacher oversight (see the approved-tools decisions in Appendix A above).

### Learners with additional needs

Three groups need explicit decisions beyond the grade bands above:

- **Students with IEPs or 504 plans.** Whether AI counts as an accommodation is a decision for the **IEP/504 team**, documented in the plan — the school-wide policy never overrides it. US federal guidance requires IEP teams to consider assistive technology at every plan, and districts must provide decided supports at no cost; where the team decides AI serves as assistive technology, the same process applies. Caution in the other direction too: staff must never enter IEP content or student records into public AI tools when drafting plans (see §2.6).
- **Under-13 learners.** US law (COPPA, as amended 2025) requires verifiable parental consent before online services collect personal information from children under 13 — schools may consent on parents' behalf only where the data stays entirely within educational use, with no commercial purpose. Most consumer AI tools set a 13+ minimum in their own terms and are not built for school consent. This is the legal reason behind the elementary teacher-led rule above: young students use AI only through school-managed tools or teacher-led activity, never personal accounts on public platforms.
- **Multilingual and advanced learners.** Translation and read-aloud are access, not shortcuts (see Part 1, Domain 5) — name them as approved uses. For advanced learners, AI-Building on approved platforms can be extension work, not just enrichment.

### Quick tool-review checklist (for staff, before introducing any AI tool)
Ask these five questions before a tool reaches students:

| Question | Yes / No / Notes |
|---|---|
| Is the tool school- or district-approved? | |
| Is it age-appropriate, with **no student account required**? | |
| Does it protect student privacy and avoid collecting personal data? | |
| Does it support our learning goals? | |
| Has the output been reviewed before sharing it with students? | |

### A privacy/safety baseline (grade-banded, adapt to your context)
Public AI tools may store or learn from what is typed into them. Set a schoolwide baseline of what is **never entered into AI**, and let classrooms extend it:

- **All grades:** no private information about yourself or others — no full names, addresses, or phone numbers; no passwords or school IDs; no photos of classmates.
- **Middle/high school additions:** no medical information; no confidential school information; no other person's writing entered without permission.
- **Higher-education / dual-credit additions (if applicable):** no unpublished research data, interview transcripts, patient/client information, copyrighted readings beyond fair use, or institutional documents.

State the baseline plainly in the policy, then add the school's own items. Equity matters: the baseline protects every student equally, and **approved, school-managed tools** are the safe alternative that keeps students away from public, unmanaged tools.

### School-level questions to settle
- Which **approved tools** does the school provide and maintain, so students are not pushed toward public tools?
- Who reviews tools before approval — and do they use the checklist above?
- If students may **build** their own AI (chatbots, agents — the AI-Building level, Part 3), on which approved, privacy-first platforms may they do it, and who reviews the bots before they are shared with the class?
- Is a privacy slip treated as a **teachable moment** (a conversation, like any other integrity issue — see Part 3 §3.4), while repeated or harmful misuse follows the conduct code?

## 2.5 Approving and maintaining the tools list

The checklist (§2.4) judges one tool. This process keeps the whole list trustworthy over time.

**Intake.** Any staff member may propose a tool — and IT flags newly activated AI features inside already-licensed suites (e.g., assistants embedded in office or classroom platforms) — by stating: what it is, what learning purpose it serves, what student data (if any) it collects, its age minimum, and its cost/license.

**Review.** A small panel — IT lead, a principal, a teacher representative — reviews each proposal against an extended checklist: the §2.4 five questions, plus data retention and deletion terms, sub-processors, age and consent compliance (see §2.4), accessibility, and cost. Record the decision and its date.

**Decision.** Approve, pilot (time-boxed, with an evaluation question), or deny — and publish the list where staff can find it. Teachers may only introduce approved tools to students.

**Maintenance.** Re-review the list annually; re-review a single tool immediately when its terms, ownership, or data practices change. Suspend a tool at once if an incident (§2.6) implicates it.

## 2.6 Responding when AI causes harm

Most AI problems are teachable moments (Part 3 §3.4) or conduct issues. A few are safety incidents. Decide in advance which is which:

- **Tier 1 — Teachable moment.** Accidental privacy slips, first-time undisclosed use. Response: conversation, re-teaching, documentation in the classroom. (Already covered in §2.4 and Part 3.)
- **Tier 2 — Conduct process.** Repeated or deliberate misuse, including academic dishonesty. Response: the school's existing conduct code, with AI plagiarism handled as Part 3 defines it.
- **Tier 3 — Safeguarding and legal escalation.** AI exposes a student to harmful content or contact; a tool suffers a breach or changes its data practices; AI is used to harass, impersonate, or create sexual imagery of another person; AI encourages self-harm or dangerous behaviour; staff discover student records were entered into a public AI service. Response, in the first hour: contain (stop the tool's classroom use), inform the safeguarding/data lead and principal, document what happened (what, when, who, which tool, what data), contact the vendor (request deletion — noting providers may be unable to fully remove data already used to train models), notify affected families per local breach-notification rules, and suspend the tool pending review. One voice communicates outward, ideally the principal.

Keep a one-page contact sheet with this section: safeguarding lead, data/privacy owner, legal counsel, vendor contacts, and the local breach-notification reference. Review it in every policy cycle.

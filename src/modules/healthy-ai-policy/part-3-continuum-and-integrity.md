## 3.1 The AI Integration Continuum

The continuum lets teachers build units that are not an all-or-nothing choice. A healthy unit deliberately mixes levels depending on the learning goal: some lessons No-AI (so students build and show foundational skill), others AI-Resistant (so AI cannot do the work), others AI-Optional (so students practise the conscious choice of when AI is worth using), others AI-Required (so students learn to use and critique AI), a few AI-Collaborative (so students manage a deep working partnership), and — where the school's approved platform allows it — a few AI-Building (so students design, build, and refine their own AI). Deciding *in advance* which level each task needs is the mark of a maker mindset.

**Teachers decide; the policy communicates.** Teachers are the decision-makers: they decide **how, when, and why** AI appears in each lesson and assignment. The policy's job is to **communicate** that mix clearly — to students, parents, and the wider staff — so classrooms stay places of professional judgement rather than rulebooks.

### The six levels (adopt these definitions as written)

**No-AI** — An assignment designed so that AI is not part of the work. The task simply does not involve AI by design, so the student's skill and thinking stand alone. *(Examples: first drafts, reflections, observations, handwriting a response.)*

**AI-Resistant** — An assignment designed so the work **cannot be completed by AI alone**. *(Examples: an in-class oral defence, a hand-drawn artefact, a live demonstration tied to materials the student produced earlier.)*

**AI-Optional** — An assignment where **AI may be used at the student's discretion**, within boundaries the teacher sets. The student decides *whether, when, and how much* to use AI — including choosing not to — and **explains the choice** as part of the submission. *(Example: a research task where AI support is available, and students decide — and justify — their use or non-use.)*

**AI-Required** — An assignment that **expects students to use AI as a tool** and then **critique, extend, or build on** its output. The submitted artefact must include **both the AI's contribution and the student's judgment about it.**

**AI-Collaborative** — An assignment where **AI is a working partner**. The student decides *moment-to-moment* when to lean on AI and when to set it aside, and **explains that decision as part of the submission.**

**AI-Building** — An assignment where **the work is the AI itself**: students **design, build, test, and refine their own AI** — a chatbot, agent, or small model — on a school-approved, privacy-first platform (e.g., an open-source private workspace). The student is the **author of the tool**, not just its user: they document design choices, test the system against real questions, identify its errors and bias, and improve it. This is the maker mindset in its most literal form — the student shapes AI as material. *(Example: build a study-buddy chatbot for a unit; test it with classmates' questions; fix what it gets wrong; submit the build, a test log, and a reflection. See Part 2 §2.4 for approved-platform and review decisions.)*

### The distinction between No-AI and AI-Resistant (teach this to staff)

| | No-AI | AI-Resistant |
|---|---|---|
| **Why** | The task doesn't involve AI by design; skill must stand alone | Designed so AI *can't* complete it, even if a student tried |
| **Protects** | Foundational skill-building and honest self-assessment | Shows transfer and personal ownership |
| **Example** | A first draft, a reflection, an observation | An in-class oral defence, a hand-drawn artefact, a live demo |
| **Question it answers** | "Do I want AI absent from this task?" | "Is this task truly impossible for AI to fake?" |

A task can be both No-AI *and* AI-Resistant (e.g., a hand-drawn artefact produced in class without any tool). What matters is that teachers name the level so the expectation is explicit.

## 3.2 Lesson examples at each level

Walk your staff through concrete examples so the continuum is actionable, not abstract.

### No-AI example
- **Task:** First free-write of a personal narrative; a maths problem solved step-by-step on paper; a lab observation recorded in a journal.
- **Teacher's framing:** "In this first draft, I want to see your thinking and your words before anything else. No AI on this one."
- **Why:** Students need un-assisted rehearsal to build the skill honestly; this also gives the teacher a baseline of genuine student work.

### AI-Resistant example
- **Task:** An in-class oral defence where a student explains the reasoning behind a project they built; a hand-drawn diagram; a live demonstration with materials they produced earlier.
- **Teacher's framing:** "You may have used AI to help you build this, but today you'll show me you understand it, live, in front of the class."
- **Why:** It verifies transfer and ownership; AI cannot stand in for the student's live explanation.

### AI-Optional example
- **Task:** A persuasive essay where students may use AI to brainstorm ideas or get feedback on a draft — but may equally choose not to. Everyone submits a short reflection naming one decision: where they chose to use (or not use) AI, and why.
- **Teacher's framing:** "This task is yours to drive. AI may help you if you choose, or you can do it all yourself — either answer is fine. I want to hear your reasoning."
- **Why:** It practises the decision the continuum exists to teach: *when is AI worth using?* Students calibrate the tool against their own skills and the task's demands, and their reasoning is assessed — not the tool use.

### AI-Required example
- **Task:** "Write a paragraph on [topic], then use a generative AI chatbot to draft an alternative. Compare them, and write a short critique of where the AI's version is strong, weak, or wrong. Submit both the AI output and your critique."
- **Teacher's framing:** "I expect you to use AI here, and your grade depends on your judgement about its output — not the output itself."
- **Why:** Teaches critical evaluation of AI and keeps the student as author of the *judgement*. The artefact includes both AI's contribution and the student's evaluation (per your definition).

### AI-Collaborative example
- **Task:** "Research [complex question] with [AI] as your working partner. Decide moment-to-moment when you lean on the AI and when you set it aside. In your submission, explain two decisions you made: one where you leaned on AI and one where you didn't, and why."
- **Teacher's framing:** "AI is your collaborator. Your job is to manage the partnership and make it clear where your thinking, and where its help, each sit."
- **Why:** This demands deep metacognitive control — the student demonstrates control over the tool (the maker/conscious pole) and explains their choices in the submission (per your AI-Collaborative definition).

### AI-Building example
- **Task:** "Build a study-buddy chatbot for [topic] on our class platform. Teach it what it needs to know, test it with real questions from classmates, and fix what it gets wrong. Submit the bot, a test log (what it handled well and where it failed), and a short reflection on one design decision you made."
- **Teacher's framing:** "Here you're not just using AI — you're making it. The quality of your tool, and what you learned from its failures, is the work."
- **Why:** This builds real understanding of how AI systems work (training data, bias, failure modes) while keeping the student firmly in charge of the thinking. Building happens on approved, privacy-first platforms only (Part 2 §2.4) — students never train bots on personal or private information.
- **A real example:** Canadian and Portuguese students built a mentor chatbot ("Apa") for the European Space Agency's Astro Pi challenge — feeding it the challenge documentation as a knowledge base and writing its system prompt themselves. Their code earned ESA flight status (see the research log; discuss with your leadership team in §3.6, Scenario 7).

### A balanced unit (illustration)
A research unit might be: **Week 1** No-AI (brainstorm + draft thesis alone) → **Week 2** AI-Optional (drafting support available; students decide and justify their choice) → **Week 3** AI-Required (draft with AI, critique it) → **Week 4** AI-Resistant (in-class oral defence of final argument). A class that also learns to build might add **Week 5** AI-Building (students build and test a study-buddy bot around the unit's key ideas). The mix tells the learning story.

## 3.3 Placing the continuum in a broader benefit/deficit context

The continuum is the *integration* axis from Part 1. It should never be read as "higher is better." A high level (AI-Collaborative) is not "better" than No-AI — they serve **different learning goals**. Some skills are best learned No-AI (foundational fluency), some AI-Resistant (demonstrating ownership), some AI-Optional (practising the *decision* about whether to use AI — a self-regulation skill), some AI-Required (learning to critique AI), some AI-Collaborative (managing deep collaboration), and some AI-Building (constructing understanding of the tool itself by authoring it). **Balance is the goal, not maximal AI use.** Revisit the Part 1 domains (cognitive, psychological, social, integrity, equity) so teachers weigh benefit and deficit together.

## 3.4 Academic integrity in an AI world: defining AI plagiarism

Academic integrity means producing work that shows your own learning and giving credit where it is due. Generative AI asks us to extend that to a new case.

### What is AI plagiarism (plain definition)
**AI plagiarism is submitting AI-generated content as your own work without disclosure, citation, or meaningful engagement** — in the same way that copying another person's words or ideas without credit is plagiarism. It also includes using AI to **paraphrase or rewrite** work to evade detection, and fabricating sources, data, or citations with AI.

Concretely, AI plagiarism includes:
- submitting an entire AI-generated essay, answer, or code as your own with no disclosure;
- copying AI output into a submission without citing or acknowledging it;
- using AI to rewrite someone else's (or your own) work to hide its origin;
- letting AI generate fake sources, data, or references and presenting them as real.

### What is NOT AI plagiarism
By contrast, healthy AI use **discloses** and builds on AI output. It is not plagiarism when a student:
- uses AI transparently (as allowed by the assignment level) and discloses it;
- keeps the essential thinking and judgement in their own hands, per the maker mindset;
- cites or describes AI's contribution in the required way (see §3.5).

The line is drawn where **the learner stops being the author of the thinking and the credit.** If a student uses AI as a material they shape and clearly names, that is maker-minded use. If they present AI's output as their own hidden authored work, that is AI plagiarism.

### Coaching note for leaders
Approach suspected AI plagiarism educationally and with fairness:
- **Clarify expectations first.** Many integrity issues are actually clarity issues — students did not know what was allowed or what disclosure looks like (the APA notes students often lack a clear definition of what constitutes cheating). Name the boundary in every assignment (Level, disclosure, citation).
- **Favour conversation over surveillance.** Detection tools are imperfect (false positives and negatives are real risks, and a detection "arms race" is corrosive to trust). Use them only to prompt a respectful conversation, and rely on AI-Resistant assessments (§3.1) for high-stakes verification.
- **Design integrity into assessment.** The most sustainable response is redesigning assessment so it reveals genuine thinking (e.g., AI-Resistant live demonstrations, process work, and in-class defence), rather than only policing output.

## 3.5 How to disclose and cite AI use

Disclosure and citation are skills, like any other — teach them rather than assume them. The module encourages students and teachers to go beyond a bare citation and **reflect on when, why, and how** they used (or deliberately did not use) AI.

### The "when, why, how" reflection (use across the school)
A simple, high-value practice is a short **AI use statement** appended to substantial work:

> **AI use statement:** I used [tool] to [task — e.g., brainstorm ideas, check grammar, draft a comparison]. I set it aside when [e.g., I needed to develop my own argument / do the maths myself]. My own thinking is in [what I judged, revised, or created]. I did not use AI for [what you must defend on your own].

This reflection does three things: it **practises honesty**, it **demonstrates a maker/conscious mindset** (you can explain your choices), and it **teaches metacognition** — which itself protects learning from reflexive offloading.

### When to cite or disclose (general rule)
As a rule of thumb: **If AI contributed meaningfully to the content or ideas you present — direct quotes or paraphrases, images or data, ideas and structures it suggested, or drafting/brainstorming/editing beyond routine grammar — acknowledge it.** Routine grammar and spelling tools generally need only a light acknowledgment, if any. Always check the teacher's and school's specific policy, which is the final authority for an assignment.

### Citation conventions (adapt to your school's style and level)
The school-wide policy should name one or two citation styles it uses and a single, plain disclosure format for younger students. If the school uses formal styles, adopt current guidance:

- **APA:** Generative AI used in drafting must be disclosed and cited (typically the tool's developer as "author," a description such as `[Generative AI chat]`, the model, date, and a shareable chat link). APA does not require citing routine tools like word processors.
- **MLA:** Cite the AI tool as a source with no author, using the prompt as the title, plus the tool, version, publisher (developer), date, and URL, so the reader can retrieve the conversation.
- **Chicago / Turabian:** Cite generative AI in footnotes, naming the tool, publisher, date, and URL; AI chat is generally not placed in the bibliography.
- **IEEE:** Disclose AI use in the acknowledgments rather than a formal reference, naming the system and how it was used.
- **Common principle:** AI is treated as a **tool/source**, never as an author (it cannot take responsibility for the work). Prefer a **shareable link to the specific conversation** where the tool allows it; include the prompt if it isn't stated in the text.

> **[Verified]** Sources reviewed this run: APA Style guidance on citing generative AI; the Editage multi-style guide (APA, MLA, Chicago, IEEE, Turabian, AMA); the Salve Regina transparency-and-citation guide; the UNLV APA/MLA citing guide; the COPE position on AI authorship. The style.mla.org page itself was not fetched directly (see log). Verify against your school's chosen style before adopting.

### School-level decisions to make
- **Which style/level?** Will your school require full formal citations (secondary), or a single plain **AI use statement** (elementary/lower levels)? Consider a two-tier system: a simple statement for all, formal citation for older students.
- **What default do we set?** Where the teacher does not specify a level, what does the student assume? (A clear default — e.g., "disclose any AI use; ask if unsure" — removes ambiguity.) Many schools set a default of **AI-Optional with disclosure**: AI is permitted unless the teacher says otherwise, and any use is disclosed and explained — the door stays open without removing accountability.
- **How do we handle editing tools?** Decide whether routine grammar/spell tools need disclosure (usually light or none) versus content-generating tools (always).

## 3.6 Scenario and role-play activities for your leadership team

Use these to pressure-test the policy before it goes live.

**Scenario 1 — The full-delegation research project.**
A teacher wants to allow a fully AI-Collaborative research project where AI plays a major role in the work. *How does your policy handle this? What must the assignment include (disclosure, the "when/why/how" statement, an AI-Resistant defence component)? Does the school-wide policy support it without undermining integrity?*

**Scenario 2 — The uncertain student.**
A student submits a polished essay but can't explain their ideas when asked. *How do you respond using conversation rather than accusation? What in your policy guides you?*

**Scenario 3 — The reflexive user.**
A student uses AI for every step of a task, without asking, because it's easier. *Which continuum level did the teacher set, and did the teacher communicate it? On an AI-Optional task, the reflexive habit becomes a coaching conversation about judgement rather than an enforcement case; on a No-AI task, it is undisclosed, off-level use. What does your policy say in each situation?*

**Scenario 4 — The parent's concern.**
A parent worries that AI use will mean their child "doesn't learn anything." *What in your policy and parent communication reassures them — and what evidence would you point to?*

**Scenario 5 — The tool that can't be faked.**
A teacher wants a high-stakes demonstration of skill. *How do you help them design an AI-Resistant assessment (oral defence, live demo, hand-drawn artefact) and explain it to students?*

**Scenario 6 — The build that went off-script.**
A student builds a class chatbot and, during testing, it repeats private information a classmate typed in earlier. *What does your AI-Building policy say about data and guardrails? Who reviews bots before they are shared with the class? How does this become the teachable moment §2.4 describes rather than a punishment?*

**Scenario 7 — The build that went to space.**
Your leadership team reviews the Sage Education case: students built a mentor chatbot ("Apa") for the ESA Astro Pi challenge — assembling the official documentation as a knowledge base, writing the system prompt themselves, and choosing a coaching stance that never spoon-fed answers; their code earned ESA flight status. *What makes this AI-Building rather than AI-Required or AI-Collaborative? Where do the maker mindset and the healthy-offloading logic show up in the students' design choices? What would your school need to replicate this on an approved, privacy-first platform, and what review process (Part 2 §2.4) applies before the bot meets the class? Where could this fail in your context — and how does Scenario 6 sharpen that picture?*

**Audience:** School IT leads + building principals/assistant principals (K–12) — who will then coach their teachers
**Format:** Self-paced · Coaching toolkit · No time limit
**Purpose:** Guide teachers to build a **classroom-level** AI policy that is **aligned to and fed by** the school-wide policy (Part 2) and the continuum + integrity core (Part 3). Because the school-wide policy comes first, every classroom draft shares the same values, definitions, and expectations — while still being made by the teacher *with* their students.

> **Sequence matters.** The school-wide policy (Part 2) is the frame. The classroom policy (this part) fills in the specifics for one class. A classroom policy must never contradict the school-wide one — it adapts it. This Part 4 shows teachers how to co-create that classroom draft with students and communicate it to parents.

---

## 4.1 Why co-create with students?

Co-creating the classroom AI norms **with** students (rather than handing them a finished list) is a maker-mindset act in itself:
- It treats students as **authors of their own learning norms**, not passive consumers of rules.
- It builds **buy-in**, because students help decide and therefore understand the reason behind each norm.
- It practises the **conscious** thinking the whole module aims for — students articulate *when, why, and how* they will use AI.
- It surfaces what students actually know and think about AI, which the teacher can then teach to.

The teacher remains the leader and final authority; students are partners in drafting. This is a guided negotiation, not a free-for-all.

## 4.2 The classroom co-creation protocol (Step 0 for staff, Steps 1–6 with students)

Give each teacher the following protocol to run with their class (adapt session length to grade level). It works for elementary through secondary; adjust language and depth.

**Step 0 — Learn it yourself first (staff session, 60–90 minutes).**
Run this with teachers *before* any classroom co-creation. Teachers cannot guide what they have not felt from the learner's seat.

1. **Taste three levels (30 min).** Each teacher completes the same small task three ways: once No-AI (draft alone), once AI-Optional (choose whether to use AI, then justify the choice), once AI-Required (use AI, then critique it). Debrief in pairs: where did the thinking live each time?
2. **Write your own AI use statement (15 min).** Each teacher appends a when/why/how statement to the task above, then swaps with a partner: *is the disclosure sufficient — would you accept this from a student?* Calibrate as a staff until "sufficient" means the same thing to everyone.
3. **Surface the anxieties (15 min).** Anonymous exit slips: *what worries you most about AI in your classroom?* Cluster them on the wall. Revisit your emotional-audit answers (Activity 1-C) — what has shifted, and what support do the worried need first?
4. **Name your starting levels (15 min).** Each teacher leaves with draft decisions for two upcoming assignments: which level, and why. These enter Step 2's classroom conversation as a starting position, not a finished verdict.

*Facilitation help: timing, room setup, and the hard-conversation playbook are in the companion `facilitation_guide.md`.*

**Step 1 — Start from the school-wide policy.**
Read together the school's AI policy (Part 2) and, in age-appropriate language, the mindset (your Activity 1-A lead pair and everyday equivalent), the definitions of AI and GAI (your §1.2 record), and the six continuum levels. Students should see that the class is *implementing* a shared school frame, not inventing its own rules from nothing.

**Step 2 — Share the teacher's decisions.**
You — the teacher — decide **how, when, and why** AI shows up in this classroom. Share those decisions plainly: which kinds of assignments will be No-AI, AI-Resistant, AI-Optional, AI-Required, AI-Collaborative, or AI-Building, and why (Part 3). Students learn that "no AI" on a task is a *learning decision*, not a punishment — and that the classroom policy, aligned to the school-wide policy, is the document that communicates this to them and their parents.

**Step 3 — Get students talking.**
Guided discussion prompts:
- *Where have you seen or used AI? What was it good at? What did it get wrong?*
- *When might using AI help you learn — and when might it keep you from learning?*
- *What is fair and honest when a tool can write your work for you?*
- *How do you want to show your own thinking even when you use AI?*

**Step 4 — Draft the norms together.**
As a class, co-write a short list of **positives** ("we promise to…") and a few **guardrails** ("we will not…"). Keep language in the students' voice. Examples:
- "We use AI to help us think, not to think for us."
- "If we use AI, we say so and we show what we changed."
- "We ask the teacher when we're not sure if AI is allowed on a task."
- "We never submit AI's words as our own."
Keep it short — 4–6 norms that everyone can remember.

**Step 5 — Agree on disclosure for the class.**
Pick the class's AI use statement format (from Part 3, §3.5 — the simple "when, why, how" statement, plus the school's chosen citation style for older students). Show a filled-in example so students know exactly what to do.

**Step 6 — Sign, share, and revisit.**
Finalise the classroom policy, have the class signal agreement, and send a parent-friendly version home (see §4.3). Commit to revisiting the norms each term and whenever something new happens with AI.

## 4.3 Communicating the classroom policy to parents

Parents often feel left out of AI-in-school decisions. A co-created classroom policy is a chance to bring them in. Give parents:

- **A plain explanation** of the school-wide and classroom AI policies (no jargon; define the mindset in your Activity 1-A words — lead pair plus everyday equivalent — along with the continuum levels and disclosure).
- **What to expect:** the kinds of assignments at each level and how AI will and won't be used in *this* class.
- **What AI use looks like here:** the "when, why, how" statement and citation approach, so a parent recognises healthy use at home.
- **What they can do at home:** encourage their child to explain their AI use, ask "what was your thinking here?", and value the process over the polished output.
- **Who to ask:** the teacher's name and how to reach out with questions.

**Sample parent letter (adapt):**

> Dear Families,
>
> At [school], we believe AI can help our students learn and create — when we use it consciously, with our own thinking in charge. We call this a **maker mindset**: students are the authors of their work, and AI is a tool they shape, not a shortcut that thinks for them.
>
> In [teacher/subject/class], we've worked with students to agree on how we'll use AI. Some assignments are **No-AI** (so students build and show their own skill). Some are **AI-Resistant** (designed so only the student can complete them). Some are **AI-Optional** (students may choose whether to use AI — and explain that choice). Some are **AI-Required** (students use AI and critique it). A few are **AI-Collaborative** (AI is a working partner, and students explain their choices). And in some classes, students may even **build** their own AI — a study chatbot they design, test, and improve on a school-approved platform.
>
> Whenever students use AI, they'll say so — and they'll reflect on *when, why, and how* they used it (or chose not to). We never want a student to submit AI's words as their own.
>
> If your child talks about AI at home, ask them, *"What was your thinking here?"* — that's the best support you can give. Please reach out to me anytime with questions.
>
> Warmly,
> [Teacher]

**Coaching note for leaders.** Equip teachers with this parent letter in your school's plain-language style. The classroom policy is translated for parents; the school-wide policy governs. Keep both consistent so parents receive one clear story. Use your Activity 1-A words for the mindset terms so the letter matches the language the class co-created in.

## 4.4 Classroom safety, privacy, and elementary-grade supports

> **[Adapted with attribution]** — The privacy norms and elementary framing in this section adapt (paraphrase, not copy) the elementary and privacy materials from Med Kharbach's *Classroom AI Use & Disclosure Agreement* (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). See `99_research_log.md` for attribution.

Health and safety norms belong in the classroom agreement, not only in the school-wide policy. Co-create them with students, starting from the school's grade-banded baseline (Part 2, §2.4). Two things need special care: **privacy** and **elementary-age framing**.

### Privacy norms to co-create with students
Begin with the school's baseline, then let the class add its own items and say them in the students' voice:

- **All grades:** we never type private information about ourselves or others into AI — no full names, no addresses or phone numbers, no passwords or school IDs, no photos of classmates.
- **Middle/high school:** add medical information, confidential school information, and other person's writing (entered without permission) to the "never" list.
- **A useful habit:** when in doubt, ask the teacher before typing anything.

Explain the *why* briefly: public AI tools may store or learn from what is typed, and private information can spread beyond the classroom. A class-written norm might be: "We never type other people's private information into AI, and we ask when we're not sure."

### Elementary-grade supports (adapt for younger classes)
Younger students need concrete frames rather than abstract policy:

- **"AI is not a person."** A simple classroom message: AI doesn't know us, care about us, or understand things the way people do — it makes answers from patterns, which is exactly why we use it carefully and never give it our private information.
- **Teacher-led use.** In most elementary classrooms AI should be teacher-led or school-approved (Part 2, §2.4). Students meet AI as a class — often on the smartboard, with the teacher — rather than alone on personal accounts.
- **The three-level stoplight** is an elementary-friendly way to teach the continuum (the AI-Building level is advanced — younger students usually only meet it as a class activity, "Use together" with the teacher):
  - **Stop** — this work is done without AI (≈ No-AI; AI-Resistant tasks are usually part of *how* the task is designed, e.g., an oral defence).
  - **Ask first** — AI may help only if the teacher says yes (≈ AI-Optional — the teacher sets the boundary, the student chooses within it).
  - **Use together** — we use AI as a class, with teacher help (≈ AI-Required or AI-Collaborative, teacher-led).
- **Short rules, spoken aloud.** Five simple rules work well: ask first; keep private things private; use your own thinking; check the answer; tell how AI helped. Read them back as a class, then sign the agreement.
- **A reflection card.** After using AI, younger students can answer three quick prompts — "AI helped me with…", "I checked my work by…", "I changed…" — as an exit ticket that practises the when/why/how reflection at their level (Part 3, §3.5).

**Coaching note for leaders.** These are scaffolds, not scripts. Give teachers the grade-banded baseline and the elementary frames, then let each class co-write the norms in its own voice. The school-wide policy stays the frame; the classroom policy is the "what this class promises."

## 4.5 Keeping the classroom and school-wide policies aligned

Alignment is maintained three ways:

1. **Same frame.** The classroom policy opens by referencing the school-wide policy and shares its definitions, mindset axis, and continuum/levels. Nothing in the classroom policy contradicts the school frame.
2. **Same disclosure standard.** The classroom uses the school's chosen disclosure/citation approach (Part 3, §3.5) — the "when, why, how" statement, plus the citation style selected schoolwide.
3. **Living documents with a shared review cycle.** When the school-wide policy is reviewed (Part 2), classroom policies are updated to match. Classroom norms are revisited each term with students. The school-wide review can ask teachers for examples of what students needed, so the top-level policy stays grounded in classroom reality.

**Reflection for your leadership team:**
- *When a school-wide policy changes, how will you prompt every teacher to bring their classroom policy into alignment?*
- *How will you share strong classroom norms across grade bands so elementary, middle, and high school speak a consistent language?*
- *How will you gather teacher and student feedback into each school-wide review — keeping the top level responsive to the bottom?*
